The rent reset: why your renewal isn’t the deal you signed.

Medical office space is nearly full, and a lease signed years ago resets to today’s market at renewal. Why the gap is the negotiation, and the three real choices you actually have.

6 minute read

In brief

  • Medical outpatient occupancy hit 92.5 percent in early 2026, and rents have outpaced general office, so a lease signed years ago now sits below today’s market.
  • At renewal your rent resets to that higher market, and a full market with expensive buildouts quietly moves leverage to the landlord.
  • You have three real choices: renew, relocate, or restructure toward ownership. Sometimes the cheapest move is simply to renew, and this piece says so.

When your lease comes up for renewal, the rent resets to what the space would cost a new tenant today, not to what you have been paying. In a market this full, that gap can be large.

When you signed years ago, you locked a rent to that year’s market. The market kept moving. At renewal, the landlord resets you to today. If today’s number is well above your in-place rent, the increase you are looking at is not the landlord being aggressive. It is the gap closing. That can feel like a penalty. It is really a reset, and it has been building the entire time you were seeing patients and not thinking about real estate.

The market moved without you

Medical office is about as full as it gets. Occupancy across the top 50 U.S. markets reached 92.5 percent in the first quarter of 2026, with absorption of 3.8 million square feet, up 71 percent from a year earlier. Occupancy set a record of 92.7 percent at the end of 2025, and rents have grown around 3.3 percent a year, outpacing general office.

You do not need all of those numbers. You need three facts, because three facts change your decision.

There are fewer empty suites to move into than there were last cycle. Rents have climbed faster than general office, which means the gap between an older in-place rent and today’s market widens every year you are not looking at it. And the same reports flag a growing spread between what sitting tenants pay and what space costs now. You are not imagining the jump, and you are not being singled out.

Why a full market hands leverage to the landlord

Your leverage at renewal is mostly one thing: your cost to leave. The tighter the market, the higher that cost, and it has gone up two ways.

The first is availability. When occupancy sits above 92 percent, there are simply fewer comparable suites to relocate into, and the ones that exist are not priced to rescue you. New space now leases for close to double what sitting tenants pay, average medical outpatient rents in the mid 20s per square foot against new construction north of 40 dollars.

The second is the cost of rebuilding. Moving a medical practice is not first and last month’s rent. It is building a clinic again, and building a clinic has gotten expensive. Industry reviews put a full clinical buildout in some markets at 80 to 100 dollars per square foot, higher than before the pandemic. The bill to start over somewhere new is bigger than it used to be.

Put those together and “we’ll just move” stops being a credible threat, and the landlord knows it. The answer is not to bluff leverage you do not have. It is to start early enough that you have real options instead of one. How to handle a renewal when moving is not realistic goes deeper on the leverage you still hold when relocating is off the table.

The three real choices

Strip away the noise and a renewal comes down to three moves. Each has a cost, and the cost belongs in the open.

Renew

Stay put. Often the right call if your rate is still reasonable and the space works.

Cost: you accept the reset. Upside: no downtime, no rebuild.

Relocate

Move on. Fresh space, fresh terms.

Cost: the buildout at today’s prices, patient disruption, thin options.

Restructure

Change the decision. Put a path to owning the space on the table.

Cost and tradeoff named below.

The honest alternatives

Before any of that, two things are worth saying, because they are true more often than a real estate company usually admits.

If your in-place rent is still close to market and the space fits, just renew. The reset is mild, the cheapest move is to sign, and the best use of your attention is your practice, not a negotiation you do not need to have.

And if you have the capital and the certainty, buying your building outright can be the cheaper move, and we would say so. A lease with a purchase option is built for owners who would rather keep their capital in the practice right now, not for owners who can comfortably own today. If that is you, own it. We make the fuller case in When owning your first building is actually the cheaper move.

There is also a timing case. If you are within a couple of years of selling or leaving, a long commitment of any shape may be the wrong fit, and a shorter, more flexible renewal can be worth paying a little more for.

Where a lease with an option fits

For the owner who does not want to renew forever and is not ready to buy, there is a third path. An investor buys or holds the building. You lease it at fair market rent plus a small premium. And you hold a written option to buy at year 5 or year 10, priced by an independent appraisal. Your capital stays in the practice, and your name stays off a mortgage.

The tradeoff, plainly

  • The premium is real money, paid for keeping the choice open. If you never exercise the option, that is what it bought.
  • Today’s price is not locked. The appraisal at exercise sets it, up or down.
  • The lease still includes a personal guarantee, like any commercial lease. What you avoid is the mortgage and the down payment.

Whether that trade is worth it is a math question, not a marketing one. It pencils for some practices and not for others. For how to interrogate any option, ours included, see How to tell whether a purchase option is real.

What to do now

Start 24 to 30 months before your lease expires. Leverage at renewal is mostly a function of how early you started, and the owner who begins with two years of runway negotiates from a different position than the one who opens the letter with 90 days left. The renewal clock starts two years early lays out the calendar.

  • Get an honest read on market rent for your space, so you know the size of the gap before the landlord tells you.
  • Model all three paths side by side, and put the real cost of a buildout at today’s prices into the relocate column so the comparison is fair.
  • Stress-test the practice against a meaningful rent step-up. If the numbers do not survive it, that is something you want to learn now, with time to act.

A full market is not automatically bad news for your practice. It is only bad news if the first time you look at the gap is the month the renewal shows up.
